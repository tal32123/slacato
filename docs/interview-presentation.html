01 · Business value
00:00 – 01:30

The hour before the negotiation.

An account owner walks into a strategic negotiation holding five things that do not live together: the CRM record, the last calls, what the account team said in chat, the pricing position, and the Deal Desk policy that governs what may be offered. Assembling that by hand is slow, and the parts that get skipped are the parts that are hardest to find — the conflicting signal in a chat thread, the policy clause that makes a discount someone else's decision.

The deals are large

The three fixture opportunities carry annual contract values of $4.22M, $2.38M and $1.88M. A preparation gap on a deal this size is not a minor cost.

The context is split by permission

Six users, five source types, and per-user flags for sensitive pricing and restricted-account access. Not every seller may see every signal on their own deal.

The commercial position is already contested

OPP-1003 carries an 18% requested discount with an −8% renewal uplift, marked pending_deal_desk, and an alternative at 12% marked pending_legal_and_manager.

What changes for the account owner

They ask for one deal and get back a nine-section brief in which every factual sentence carries a citation that names the source file and the stable record ID, in the format the assignment asked for — source=slack/account_team_updates.tsv, update_id=SLK-9007. Anything the Deal Desk policy makes someone else's decision is not quietly written into customer-facing language; the run stops and waits for the named approver.

The goal is not to negotiate for the seller. It is to make the preparation reviewable: the seller can see what the assistant knew, where it got it, and what it was not allowed to say yet.

Speaker notes · 1:30

Cato's problem here is not that the data is missing. It is that it is scattered, and it is scattered across systems with different access rules. Before a negotiation, an account owner has to reconcile the CRM record, the last few Gong calls, whatever the account team said in chat, the pricing position, and the Deal Desk policy that governs what they are even allowed to offer. That takes time they do not have, and the pieces that get dropped are exactly the awkward ones — the update where two executives disagreed, the policy clause that makes a discount someone else's call.

These are not small deals. In the fixture data the three opportunities are worth four point two million, two point four million and one point nine million in annual contract value, and the restricted one already has an eighteen percent discount requested against a negative renewal uplift, sitting pending at Deal Desk. That is precisely the situation where walking in under-prepared is expensive.

So what the assistant does is narrow. You give it an opportunity and who you are. It gathers only the evidence you personally are allowed to see, and it hands back one brief where every factual sentence names the file and the record ID it came from. And where the policy says a human owns the decision — a discount over fifteen percent, a liability cap change, customer-facing concession language — it does not write that language. It stops and routes it to the person who owns it. I am not trying to replace the seller's judgement. I am trying to make the preparation something a manager can audit.

02 · Design choices
01:30 – 04:30

Seven decisions I would defend.

Three minutes is roughly twenty-five seconds each. Say the decision and the reason; the detail is in the technical overview and I should let them ask for it.

1 · Four agents, split by evidence type rather than by step

Conversation Intelligence, Stakeholder Map, and Commercial Analysis each read a different slice of the evidence and return a different typed object; Negotiation Strategy consumes all three and produces the brief. The split is by what the agent has to be good at reading, not by pipeline stage, which is why the three specialists can run concurrently and why one of them failing degrades the brief rather than ending the run.

packages/core/src/application/agents/{conversation,stakeholder,commercial,strategy}.ts

2 · The agents have no tools at all

The shared agent input type deliberately exposes no repository, transport, tool or other-agent handle — it carries the run, the account, the opportunity, the evidence manifest, the recomputed current scope, and the evidence itself. This is a security decision before it is a design one: an agent that cannot fetch anything cannot widen its own permissions, so the authorization decision taken before generation is the one that holds, and a prompt injection inside evidence has no second path to the data.

packages/core/src/application/agents/contracts.ts — AgentContext

3 · A persisted state machine, not a free-form agent loop

Ten run states with an exhaustive transition table: created → retrieving → specialists_running → synthesizing → validating → awaiting_approval → finalizing → completed, plus rejected, failed and cancelled. An invalid transition throws rather than being tolerated, and finalizing deliberately accepts only completion or failure, so no agent output can restart synthesis. I chose this over a planner loop because approvals need a state that can outlive the process holding it.

packages/core/src/domain/runs/state-machine.ts

4 · Permissions enforced twice, and the manifest is frozen between

Grants are checked before retrieval, so unauthorized rows never enter the candidate pool; the run's authorized evidence is then frozen into a manifest, and the scope is recomputed and rechecked before evidence reaches a model, so narrowing someone's access mid-run cannot be bypassed by reusing an older manifest. The authorization lookup that maps opportunity to account and requester is recorded as its own trace span, distinct from evidence retrieval.

packages/core/src/application/agents/contracts.ts (currentScope) · authorization_lookup span in samples/restricted-run-trace.json

5 · Retrieval is hybrid, fused, then only gently adjusted

Lexical and semantic candidate lists are combined with reciprocal rank fusion at k = 60. Source reliability and recency then adjust the fused score, but each adjustment is hard-capped at ±0.02, and policy evidence is exempt from the age penalty because a policy does not get less true with time. Chunking follows each source's own structure — one record per Salesforce row, one update per Slack row, six-turn windows with one-turn overlap for transcripts.

The caller's query is fused with six fixed per-section queries, so the retriever assembles a standard evidence pack for the nine brief sections rather than answering one question. That is the right shape for this product and it is also the cause of the retrieval evaluation's red gate — I cover that honestly in the evaluation section rather than presenting it as a free choice.

packages/core/src/application/evidence/rrf.ts, retriever.ts (sectionQueries), chunk.ts

6 · Approval routing is derived from policy, not decided by a model

The commercial agent describes the position; deterministic code maps it to policy triggers and required authorities. On OPP-1003 that produced four separate approval entries requiring four distinct authorities, one of which — the account owner's customer-concession gate — declares depends_on the other three and only becomes operable once they clear. The export endpoint returned 404 while the run was still awaiting_approval.

samples/restricted-approval-flow.json · policy rules in fixtures/cato/policies/deal_desk_policy.md

7 · Every claim is re-checked against frozen evidence, and the run leaves a trace

The agent returns each claim with a citation ID; code matches it back to the frozen manifest, restores the trusted source label and locator, and drops claims that are missing, unauthorized, or unsupported by the cited text. The restricted run exported 33 spans across 13 span kinds and 15 durable events, covering authorization, retrieval, each model call, validation, guardrails, approval requirements and decisions, and finalization.

packages/core/src/application/agents/validation.ts · samples/restricted-run-trace.json, samples/restricted-run-events.json

Speaker notes · 3:00

Seven decisions, quickly, and then I will show you them running.

First, four agents, split by what they have to read rather than by pipeline stage. Conversation intelligence reads calls and chat. Stakeholder mapping reads contacts and who spoke. Commercial analysis reads pricing and policy. Strategy reads the three of them and writes the brief. That split lets the three specialists run at the same time, and it means one of them failing degrades the brief rather than killing the run.

Second, and this is the one I would most want to defend: the agents have no tools. Not few — none. The shared input type exposes no repository, no transport, no handle to another agent. The workflow hands each specialist a permission-checked bundle of evidence and takes back a typed object, and that is the entire interface. I did that for security, not elegance. An agent that cannot fetch anything cannot widen its own permissions, so the authorization decision I make before generation is the one that actually holds, and a prompt injection buried in a transcript has nowhere to go.

Third, orchestration is a persisted state machine, ten states with an exhaustive transition table, not a free-form planner loop. I chose that because of approvals. A run can sit in awaiting-approval for a day, across a worker restart, and it has to come back knowing exactly which step is next and which approvals are already recorded. An invalid transition throws instead of being tolerated, and the finalizing state only accepts completion or failure so no model output can restart synthesis behind my back.

Fourth, permissions are enforced twice. Before retrieval, so unauthorized rows never enter the candidate pool at all. Then the authorized evidence is frozen into a manifest, and the scope is recomputed and checked again before anything reaches a model — so if someone's access is narrowed mid-run, an older manifest cannot be replayed to get around it.

Fifth, retrieval is genuinely hybrid: lexical and semantic lists fused with reciprocal rank fusion, then reliability and recency nudge the score. Deliberately nudge — each adjustment is capped at two hundredths, and policy is exempt from the age penalty, because a policy clause does not get less true because it is old. One thing I will flag now and come back to: the caller's query is fused with six fixed per-section queries, so this retriever assembles a standard evidence pack rather than answering one question. That is right for the product and it is also why my retrieval evaluation is currently failing its own gate.

Sixth, what needs approval is decided by code, not by a model. The commercial agent describes the position; deterministic policy code maps it to triggers and authorities. On the restricted deal that produced four separate approvals across four distinct people, and the last one is explicitly dependent on the other three.

Seventh, grounding and observability. Every claim comes back with a citation ID, and code matches it against the frozen manifest, restores the trusted label, and drops anything unsupported. The restricted run left thirty-three trace spans across thirteen kinds and fifteen durable events. You do not have to trust the brief; you can read what produced it.

The retrieval quality gate is red, and it is reported rather than tuned away. The floor is 0.50 macro recall@5 and measured recall is 0.222. I did not lower the floor to make it green.

Retrieval: what the wider golden set exposed

The golden set is 23 cases — 18 scored cases carrying 56 hand-checked relevance labels, plus 5 that must return nothing because the requester is unauthorized. Three cases also carry forbiddenEvidenceIds: evidence a specific reader must never be handed, such as a sensitive-pricing note for a persona without that grant. Returning one counts as permission leakage exactly like answering a denied request. Leakage is 0 across all 23; the 5 denied cases retrieved 0 rows each.

The macro means cover the 18 scored cases only. The denied cases are excluded, because their correct answer is an empty list and scoring them 1.0 would flatter the number.

An earlier three-case version of this set scored 0.50 recall. That was not a better retriever — it was a smaller set whose handful of labels happened to be the lexically obvious Slack rows. Widening it exposed two specific defects:

  • No Gong transcript chunk reaches the top 5 in any case. Not one, across all 23. Transcript windows are indexed, chunked and citable — the shipped briefs cite them — but the ranked hybrid never surfaces one in the evaluated top 5.
  • The returned set is nearly identical across unrelated queries for the same opportunity. The ranked hybrid fuses the caller's query with six fixed per-section queries, so the caller's own words carry roughly a seventh of the fusion mass. That is a defensible design for assembling a standard evidence pack for a brief, which is what the product does, and it is poor at answering a specific question, which is what this evaluation asks.

Eight of the 18 scored cases return zero relevant evidence at k=5. I would not describe this retriever as good; I would describe it as one that assembles a consistent evidence pack and does not yet answer targeted questions, with a measurement that says exactly that.

Where the output is thin

These are visible in the shipped artifacts and I would rather name them than let a reviewer find them.

  • One recommended next action per brief. All three shipped briefs produced exactly one. The assignment asks for prioritized next actions, plural; one is not much of a priority list.
  • Missing Information came back empty. The restricted brief's Missing Information section reads "None", which is almost certainly wrong for a deal with an unapproved discount and undocumented exception handling.
  • Stakeholder goals and concerns are empty. The map identifies five people with roles, influence and relationship, but their per-person goals and concerns are all "None".

Not measured at all

Latency percentiles under load. Cost in currency — token counts are recorded per run, but never converted. Load and soak behaviour. Adversarial prompt-injection results at scale. And the one that matters most commercially: whether a seller who used the brief prepared better. No human-usefulness evaluation exists.

An earlier measurement I disproved

An earlier version of the Slack impact report concluded that Slack evidence was retrieved, ranked and prompted but never reached a finalized brief. That was an accurate measurement of the code at the time. Two fixes falsified it: one capped the Gong-transcript candidate window so transcripts stopped crowding Slack out of the prompt context, and one fixed a prose safety filter that was matching role: without a word boundary — every Slack row carries an authorRole: field, so claims quoting a Slack update were being discarded as instruction-like text. The report was rewritten against the current code and says so.

And every claim carries confidence one point zero — that is a self-reported field in the schema with no calibration behind it, so I treat it as not measured rather than as high confidence.

What I have not measured at all: latency under load, cost in actual currency, soak behaviour, injection resistance at scale, and whether any of this actually helps a seller prepare better. That last one is the one I would want to fix first, and it needs sellers, not code.

One more thing, because I think it says more than the passing numbers. An earlier version of my Slack impact report concluded that Slack evidence never reached the finished brief. That was true when I measured it. Two bugs caused it — transcripts were crowding Slack out of the prompt window, and a safety filter was matching "role" without a word boundary, so every claim quoting a Slack update, which all carry an authorRole field, was being thrown away as instruction-like text. I fixed both, re-ran it, and rewrote the report to say what it now measures and what it replaces.

Speaker notes · 2:00

What would break. I want to be specific rather than list generic risks.

The recovery core is real. State is checkpointed in Postgres, worker leases expire so a dead worker's run gets picked up, commands are idempotent, and version checks stop two workers committing conflicting state. If a process dies mid-run you do not lose the brief and you do not re-ask for approvals. I would stand behind that part.

The operating envelope around it is not there. Scalability: I have separated the API from the worker and bounded each run, but I have never load-tested or soak-tested it, so I genuinely do not know where it falls over — and I would rather say that than guess a number. Vector search is exact today, which is right at this corpus size; at millions of rows in one authorized scope it needs an approximate index, though I would check what a user's permission filter actually leaves before assuming that, because exact search over a small authorized subset can be both faster and safer.

High availability: one Postgres, one Redis, no standby, and no failover or restore drill. Backups you have not restored are not backups.

Monitoring is the honest gap, and it is the one I would close first. The system already records everything an operator would want — every run leaves a full timeline — and none of it is exported anywhere. No dashboards, no alerts on queue depth or provider failures, no service objectives, no runbooks. Right now someone has to already suspect a problem to find one.

Secrets are server-side and redacted from logs, but they are long-lived keys in environment files. Production needs a secret manager or workload identity, rotation, and a revocation path you have rehearsed.

Cost: I record real token counts per run and never convert them to money. There is no budget, no spend alert, no anomaly detection. And model routing is a configuration decision, not a measured one — I have not compared quality and price per agent task, so I am not going to claim cost-aware routing that I did not evaluate.

Last, model dependence. Quality is tied to whichever model is configured, and I have not measured quality across models, so a provider or version change is an unquantified quality change. There is also no degraded mode — if the provider is down there is no cached path that still gives the seller something useful. For a tool people rely on before a negotiation, that is a real gap.

07 · Contingency
Not in the 15:00

If the live call is slow or fails.

Only Scenario A is live. Every other scenario already reads a checked-in artifact, so a provider outage costs at most one scenario — and that scenario has a recorded twin.

Rule 1 · Set the expectation first

Before pressing Generate, say the number: "the shipped runs took about thirty seconds." A wait that was announced is a demonstration; the same wait unannounced is a failure.

Rule 2 · Hard cutoff at 06:15

If the run has not finished 105 seconds into Scenario A, stop waiting and switch. Do not narrate a spinner into the next section's time.

Rule 3 · Never restart it on stage

Leave the failed run open. It is evidence: a failed run is a recorded state with a durable event trail, and the fallback becomes a demonstration of the failure handling.

Rule 4 · Say what happened

"That is a provider timeout, which is exactly why the run is checkpointed and the failure is recorded rather than swallowed. Here is the same brief from a run I recorded on the thirty-first."
